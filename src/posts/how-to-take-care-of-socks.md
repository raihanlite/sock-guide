How do you make socks last? Wash them cold and inside out, air-dry them, and give each pair a day off. That is most of it.

A sock's life is decided less by what it is made of than by what happens to it after you take it off. The same pair can last a few months or a couple of years, depending on the wash, the drying and how often it gets a day off.

None of the habits below are complicated. They are small and repeatable, and they add up to socks that keep their fit, their cushioning and their comfort for far longer.

Still choosing which socks to own? Start with [What Are Socks? Why Do We Wear Socks?](/posts/what-are-socks-why-do-we-wear-socks/) and [Types of Socks](/posts/types-of-socks/) — this guide is about keeping the pairs you already have alive.

## Washing: cold, inside out, gentle

Most sock damage happens in the washing machine, and most of it is avoidable. The routine that makers and care guides agree on is simple:

- **Turn socks inside out before washing.** The inside carries the sweat, body oils and dead skin — the surface you want the water and detergent to reach.
- **Use cool water, 30–40°C, on a gentle or delicate cycle.** Cool water cleans socks perfectly well.
- **Use a mild detergent, and skip the fabric softener.** Softener coats the fibres and destroys the elastic grip that keeps a sock up.
- **Wash with similar things.** Don't wash wool socks with fuzzy towels, or with anything that has an open zipper — close zippers first.
- **Sort before you wash, not after.** Match pairs and check for holes while you turn them inside out.

### Why each step matters

Turning them also protects the outer face from abrasion against zippers, buttons and other garments in the drum. That abrasion is what causes pilling and fading.

Sweat and body oils dissolve readily at moderate temperatures, and modern detergents are made to work in cold water.

Hot water shrinks wool, weakens elastic and fades colours. For merino wool specifically, stay at 30°C or below and use low agitation: hot water lifts the fibre's cuticle scales, and once wool felts there is no way back.

Softener also blocks the moisture-wicking channels in performance socks, and leaves a residue that makes odour worse over time. Bleach is out for wool — it damages the fibres.

The fuzz from towels attaches to wool, and an open zipper snags and can make a hole. A mesh laundry bag is worth it for good socks: it contains them and reduces mechanical stress in the drum.

It is far less frustrating than discovering a missing sock or a hole after everything is clean and dry.

If you only need to wash socks, hand-washing saves water and is gentler still: cool water, a little mild detergent, a few minutes' soak, no wringing — press the water out instead.

## Drying: air-dry wins

Heat is the second enemy. The high heat of a tumble dryer breaks down natural fibres prematurely and damages the elastic that keeps a sock fitting — which is why the standard advice for good socks is to hang dry and never use the dryer. In practice:

- **Air-dry whenever you can.** Hang socks by the cuff or peg them on a line or rack, in moving air. They dry fast — most pairs are dry within a few hours indoors.
- **Lay wool socks flat to dry.** Hanging a wet wool sock lets gravity pull it out of shape; laid flat it keeps its shape and fit. The same applies to cashmere and other delicate knits.
- **If you must tumble dry, use low heat.** Most cotton and synthetic socks tolerate a low setting, and most merino can take low heat too.

Air drying extends the life of every sock, and low is the ceiling, never high.
- **Never put a wet sock away.** A damp sock in a drawer stays damp, smells, and grows the bacteria that cause odour. Make sure pairs are fully dry before they go back in the drawer.

## Rotation and storage

A sock worn two days running has not dried or recovered its cushioning, and it shows in odour and wear.

Give each pair a day's rest between wears: the fibres relax, the elastic recovers, and any remaining moisture evaporates.

A rotation of at least one pair per day of the week for your main activity — work, sport, everyday — is the cheapest comfort upgrade there is. It roughly doubles the life of each pair compared with wearing favourites back-to-back.

Storage matters too. Fold pairs flat or roll them loosely — don't ball them, which stretches the cuff out of shape. Keep the drawer dry: damp storage is where odour and mildew start, and cedar sachets help absorb moisture and deter moths from wool. And keep your toenails trimmed: untrimmed toenails are one of the most common causes of holes at the end of a sock, and the easiest to prevent.

## Fixing small holes

A small hole does not always mean the end. If the hole is isolated and the fabric around it is still strong, darning is worth doing: a darning egg (or any smooth rounded object) stretched inside the sock, a needle and matching thread, woven across the hole until it is filled. The repair must lie flat and smooth — a thick knot under a pressure area will irritate the foot and can cause a blister where the hole never did.

Don't repair when the surrounding fabric is thin, or when the yarn around the hole breaks easily under a gentle pull: one patched hole in a dying sock just moves the failure to the next weak spot. And if the same spot keeps wearing through, check the shoe as well as the sock — a rough seam, a damaged lining or a worn insole can act like sandpaper, and replacing the sock without fixing the shoe repeats the same hole.

## When to replace

Socks are consumables, not permanent kit — and they usually stop doing their job before any hole appears. Check for these signs:

- **Thinning at the heel or toe.** Hold the sock up to the light: if the heel or forefoot is noticeably thinner, or you can see your fingertip through it, the cushioning is already gone.
- **A cuff that no longer recovers.** Pull the cuff to roughly double its resting width and release it. A healthy cuff snaps back firmly; a dead one returns slowly, incompletely, or not at all.
- **Odour that survives washing.** Lingering smell after a proper wash means the fibres have broken down and can no longer release moisture effectively.
- **Bunching and slipping.** If a sock keeps gathering under the foot after you have positioned the heel correctly, it has lost its shape — or it never fitted that shoe and foot combination well.
- **Rough, heavily pilled inner surface.** Some pilling is normal surface wear and does not mean a sock is finished — look beneath the pills for thinning or broken yarns.

### What each sign means

The heel and toe wear first — they take the most friction inside the shoe.

A dead cuff means that sock will slide down all day, and the folds it makes underfoot increase friction and blister risk.

But when the inner surface has become noticeably rougher than when new, the fabric is working against comfort rather than for it.

As a rough guide to lifespan, judged by condition rather than the calendar alone: everyday socks worn several times a week last about 6–12 months; athletic and work socks, which take more friction and moisture, about 3–6 months; dress socks worn occasionally, 12–18 months.

Compression socks lose their graduated pressure as the elastic fatigues, so they need the strictest turnover — around 3–6 months if you rely on them.

## FAQ
<details class="faq"><summary>How often should I wash socks?</summary><div class="a">After every wear. Socks absorb sweat, oils and dead skin all day, and wearing them a second day without washing means putting a damp, bacteria-loaded sock back on your foot. The one exception is a lightly worn merino pair on a cool, low-activity day — merino's odour resistance means it can sometimes go a second day — but daily washing is the safe default for every other sock.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Can I put socks in the dryer?</summary><div class="a">Yes on low heat, but air-drying is better. High dryer heat breaks down natural fibres and damages the elastic that keeps a sock fitting, so it shortens the life of every pair. Wool socks should be laid flat to air-dry rather than hung or tumbled, so they keep their shape.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Should I wash socks inside out?</summary><div class="a">Yes — it is the single most useful habit. The inside of the sock is the dirtiest surface, so turning it out exposes it to the water and detergent, while the outer face is protected from abrasion against zippers and other garments in the drum. It reduces pilling and fading noticeably over a sock's life.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>How long do socks last?</summary><div class="a">It depends on use and care, not a fixed date: everyday socks worn several times a week last roughly 6–12 months, athletic and work socks 3–6 months, and occasional dress socks 12–18 months. Judge by condition, not the calendar — thinning at the heel or toe, a cuff that no longer snaps back, and odour that survives washing are the real signals.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Can I fix a hole in a sock?</summary><div class="a">A small, isolated hole in otherwise sound fabric can be darned — weave thread across it over a darning egg until it is filled, keeping the repair flat and smooth so it doesn't irritate the foot. Don't bother when the fabric around the hole is thin or breaks easily: the sock is finished, and the next hole is already on its way.</div></details>
