A sock is mostly a label you wear on your feet. The brand matters less than the fibre list on that label: the fibre decides whether your feet stay dry or turn damp, warm or cold, fresh or smelly — and how long the sock lasts before the heel thins through. Two socks can look identical and behave completely differently, because one is cotton and the other is a merino blend.

Your feet give fabric a hard test. A pair of feet has around a quarter of a million sweat glands, and inside a closed shoe that sweat has nowhere to evaporate to.

So the fabric has to do the work: take the moisture away from the skin, or store it out of the way, and keep doing it all day. This guide goes fibre by fibre — what each one does with sweat, warmth, odour, durability and care — then which fabric suits which job.

## How to read a sock label

The label lists fibres by weight, largest first. "80% cotton, 17% nylon, 3% elastane" means the sock is mostly cotton, with nylon added for strength and a little elastane for stretch. A few rules of thumb for reading it:

- **The first fibre is the main character.** It decides most of the feel and the moisture behaviour. Everything after it is there to fix its weaknesses.
- **Nylon or polyester in the mix usually means reinforcement.** Look for it at the heel and toe, where socks wear through first.
- **Elastane (spandex) is almost always a small number — 1 to 5%.** That is normal and enough; a bigger number does not mean a better sock, and a sock with none at all will bag out and slide down.
- **"100%" of anything is a statement, not a virtue.** A 100% cotton sock is soft and cheap, and it will soak through on a long walk.
- **The same goes for merino.** A 100% merino sock is warm and fresh, and it will wear through faster than a blend. Most good socks are blends on purpose.

The full fibre-by-fibre comparison is in [Cotton vs Merino Wool Socks](/posts/cotton-vs-merino-wool-socks/) for the two most common natural fibres; this guide covers all of them.

## Cotton

Cotton is the default sock fibre, and the most misunderstood. It is soft, breathable when dry, cheap, easy to wash, and comfortable for short, low-activity wear — sitting at a desk, walking to the shops, wearing around the house.

It is also the worst common fibre at handling sweat. Cotton absorbs moisture into the fibre and holds it against the skin, and it dries slowly. Once wet, it stays wet, and wet cotton loses its insulation and feels cold and clammy.

That is why hikers have a saying about cotton in cold weather — wet cotton is a liability outdoors, even if the stakes in a sock are lower than in a jacket.

The dampness has a second cost: wet fabric against skin increases friction, and friction on damp skin is how blisters start. Cotton does not cause blisters on its own, but on a long walk, a long shift or a run, it is the most common avoidable cause. Cotton also holds odour once it stays damp, because bacteria thrive in the warm, wet environment it creates.

None of this makes cotton a bad fibre — it makes it a fibre for the right job. Short, dry, everyday wear is cotton's home. For anything active, long, or sweaty, it needs help from a blend, or a different fibre entirely. A cotton-polyester blend is the usual compromise: cheaper than wool, dries faster than pure cotton, and keeps cotton's softness.

## Merino wool

Merino is the fibre other sock fibres are measured against. It comes from merino sheep, and its fibres are fine — fine enough to bend against the skin instead of prickling it, which is why merino socks are comfortable for people who cannot tolerate ordinary wool.

Its signature trick is moisture. A merino fibre has a water-repelling outer cuticle and a water-attracting inner core, so it stores moisture vapour inside the fibre — up to around 30% of its own weight before it feels wet.

Your foot sweats, the fibre takes the moisture up, and your skin keeps feeling dry for hours. When that stored moisture eventually evaporates, it cools the foot, which is why thin merino works in summer as well as winter.

The same crimped structure that traps air for warmth also means merino keeps insulating when damp, where wet cotton stops insulating at all.

Merino is also naturally odour-resistant: the dry environment it creates and the chemistry of the fibre limit the bacteria that cause foot odour, so merino socks can be worn longer between washes than cotton or synthetics.

The trade-offs are price and durability. Pure merino wears faster at the heel and toe than a blend, and it costs more. That is why most merino socks are blends — the classic sock yarn is around 75% merino and 25% nylon, with the nylon carrying the mechanical load at the wear points.

And merino needs gentler care: a cool or warm wash, ideally inside out, and air drying rather than a hot tumble dryer, which can felt and shrink it. The full care routine is in [How to Take Care of Socks](/posts/how-to-take-care-of-socks/).

## Alpaca

Alpaca is merino's warmer, smoother cousin, and it works differently. Where merino fibres are solid and heavily crimped, alpaca fibres are often hollow or partially hollow inside — the hollow core traps air within the fibre itself, so an alpaca sock reaches a given warmth with less material.

For the same weight, alpaca runs warmer than merino, which is why it is the fibre of choice for cold, still conditions: sitting in a hide, standing on a cold floor, sleeping in a cold house.

Alpaca handles moisture the opposite way from merino. It absorbs much less — instead of storing moisture inside the fibre, it passes it along the surface and releases it, so it dries faster and never reaches the saturation point where a sock turns into a cold, wet compress.

That is an advantage in dry cold and a disadvantage in heavy sweat: on a steep climb or a long run, merino's moisture buffer is the more useful behaviour.

Alpaca is also naturally free of lanolin, the grease in raw wool that some skin reads as an itch, and its fibres are strong — alpaca socks tend to resist thinning at the heel longer than merino.

Alpaca has one real weakness: it barely stretches. The fibre has little natural crimp, so it drapes rather than springs back, and a pure alpaca sock would stretch out and lose its shape.

That is why well-made alpaca socks are almost always blends, with nylon for strength and a little elastane for recovery. If a label says 100% alpaca, expect it to bag out — the blend is the point. ## Polyester

Polyester is the workhorse synthetic. It absorbs almost no water — under 1% of its own weight — so instead of storing sweat it moves it along the fibre surface, through tiny capillary channels, to the outside of the sock where it can evaporate. That makes it the fastest-drying common sock fibre, and the reason most running and gym socks are polyester-based. It also holds its shape well and resists shrinking.

Its weakness is odour. Polyester moves sweat around without resisting the bacteria that feed on it, and without an added antimicrobial treatment it can develop a strong smell after repeated use.

Some makers add silver-ion or similar treatments to slow that down, but the effect fades with washing. Polyester can also feel plasticky next to skin compared with a natural fibre, which is why it is usually blended rather than used alone — with nylon for strength, elastane for fit, or a natural fibre for feel.

For hot, sweaty, high-output use — running, gym sessions, summer hiking — polyester is the right tool. For all-day wear where odour matters more than drying speed, merino beats it.

## Nylon

Nylon — polyamide on some labels — is the durability fibre. It is the strongest common sock fibre, with high tensile strength and excellent abrasion resistance, which is why it shows up in the heel, ball and toe of almost every well-made sock, even ones sold as wool or cotton. It absorbs very little moisture, dries quickly, and recovers its shape well after stretching.

Nylon is rarely the main fibre. Too much of it makes a sock less breathable and less soft next to the skin, and it does nothing for odour on its own.

Its job is reinforcement: the 20–30% of a merino or cotton blend that keeps the sock from wearing through at the exact spots where your foot rubs the shoe. When a sock's heel thins after a month, the problem is usually too little nylon, not too much.

In the standard 75/25 merino-nylon sock yarn, the 25% is doing the structural work while the 75% does the comfort work. For harder wear, makers raise the nylon share to 30% or more, usually as reinforcement in the heel and toe rather than through the whole sock.

## Bamboo viscose

Bamboo socks are not knitted from bamboo fibre the way linen is spun from flax. Bamboo pulp is dissolved and regenerated into a fibre called bamboo viscose — also labelled bamboo rayon — and that processing matters for what the sock actually does. The softness and moisture behaviour are real, but they come from the fibre structure created during processing, not from a property the plant passed along intact.

What bamboo viscose does well: it is silky-soft and smooth against the skin, it breathes well, and it absorbs more moisture than cotton and moves it away from the skin faster, so feet stay drier in a closed shoe.

It is a good choice for hot weather, sensitive skin, and long days in closed shoes. The odour story needs honesty: bamboo is often sold as naturally antibacterial, but after the viscose process the plant's own antimicrobial compounds are largely gone.

The real benefit is indirect — drier feet give odour-causing bacteria less to work with, so there is less smell at the end of a hot day, for an ordinary reason.

Bamboo viscose has two weaknesses. It is weaker when wet than cotton, and it is less durable overall — heels and toes thin sooner than on a wool sock, which is why the nylon content in a bamboo blend matters so much.

And it needs gentler care: wash cool, around 30°C, skip bleach and fabric softener (softener coats the fibre and cancels the moisture wicking), and air dry rather than tumble dry.

Almost every bamboo sock is a blend — typically bamboo viscose with nylon for durability and a little elastane for shape — because the pure fibre has little natural spring of its own.

## Blends: the fibre that is not a fibre

Most socks you will actually buy are blends, and that is not a compromise — it is the design. Each fibre covers another's weakness, and the blend is what makes a sock work all day instead of excelling at one thing and failing at another.

The common blends, and what each is for:

- **Merino + nylon (around 75/25):** the standard performance sock. Merino gives warmth, moisture buffering and odour control; nylon gives the heel and toe the strength to survive. Raise the nylon share for harder wear.
- **Cotton + polyester:** the cheap everyday pair. Cotton gives softness and comfort; polyester speeds up drying and adds shape retention. Better than pure cotton for anything active, still not a performance sock.
- **Cotton + nylon + elastane (for example 85/10/5):** the classic casual crew. Cotton next to skin, nylon for shape, elastane to hold the cuff up.
- **Bamboo viscose + nylon + elastane:** the soft everyday sock. Bamboo for softness and moisture, nylon for the durability bamboo lacks, elastane for fit.
- **Merino + nylon + elastane (for example 60/35/5):** the all-day performance blend. Merino for comfort and odour, nylon for durability, elastane for a secure fit that does not slide down.

When a blend disappoints, the cause is usually the ratio, not the idea: too little nylon and the heel wears through; too little elastane and the cuff sags; too much cotton in a sock sold for sport and it soaks through. The label tells you the ratio — read it before you buy.

## Elastane (spandex)

Elastane is the stretch fibre, and it is almost never the main fibre. In the US it is usually called spandex; in Europe and most other regions it is called elastane. Lycra is a brand name for it, not a different fibre.

A typical sock contains 1 to 5% elastane — casual socks around 3 to 5%, sport socks with arch support 5 to 7% — and that small share does a large job: it lets the sock stretch around your foot and then pull itself back into shape, instead of staying loose and baggy.

Without it, a cotton sock stretches out permanently after a handful of wears; with it, the sock stays snug at the ankle, does not creep down inside the shoe, and keeps its structure wash after wash.

Two things worth knowing. First, more is not better: above about 8% the sock starts to squeeze, and the extra stretch fibre wears out its recovery faster.

Second, heat is elastane's enemy — a hot wash and a hot tumble dryer make the fibres brittle and permanently weaken them, which is why a sock with a dead cuff is finished no matter how good the rest of it looks. Wash cool and air dry, and the elastane lasts as long as the rest of the sock.

And one clarification: elastane is not the same as compression. A sock can carry a small elastane percentage purely to hold its shape, with no compression benefit at all. Compression socks use much higher elastane shares and a graduated knit to apply measured pressure — the difference is explained in [What Are Compression Socks Used For?](/posts/what-are-compression-socks-used-for/).

## Which fabric for which job

- **Everyday, short and dry:** cotton, or a cotton-polyester blend. Cheap, soft, and the sweat never gets bad enough to matter.
- **Sport, running and the gym:** polyester or a polyester-nylon blend, with elastane for fit. Fastest drying, so it blisters least. If odour bothers you, a merino blend is the alternative — slower to dry, fresher for longer.
- **Hiking and long days on your feet:** merino or a merino-nylon blend. The moisture buffer and the odour resistance are what you pay for, and the nylon keeps the heel intact.
- **Cold and still:** alpaca or an alpaca blend. Warmest per weight, and the fast drying matters less when you are not sweating hard.
- **Sweaty feet:** merino first, bamboo viscose second, performance synthetics third — and never pure cotton.
- **Dress and office:** thin merino or a fine cotton blend, in a dark colour. Merino for all-day comfort, cotton for a short day in a cool office.
- **Sensitive skin:** bamboo viscose or fine merino. Both are smooth against the skin; bamboo is the softer of the two, merino the more durable.
- **On a budget, or hard on socks:** a blend with a real nylon share — cotton-polyester for everyday, merino-nylon for performance. Pure anything is either more expensive or shorter-lived than the equivalent blend.

If you remember one thing, make it this: the fibre is the sock's job description. Cotton stores sweat, merino buffers it, polyester moves it, nylon survives it, bamboo softens it, and elastane holds the whole thing in shape. Read the label, match the fibre to the job, and most sock problems disappear before you buy.

Alpaca is warmest per weight for cold, still conditions. Cotton is best only for short, dry, everyday wear. For most people, a blend beats any pure fibre.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Is bamboo viscose good for socks?</summary><div class="a">Yes, for hot weather, sensitive skin, and long days in closed shoes. <details class="faq"><summary>What does elastane do in socks?</summary><div class="a">Elastane (spandex in the US; Lycra is a brand) gives a sock stretch and recovery. <details class="faq"><summary>Are merino socks worth the extra money?</summary><div class="a">If your socks get wet in use — work, sport, hiking, travel, long days — usually yes: you are paying for dry-feeling feet, fewer blisters and longer wear between washes. <details class="faq"><summary>Why do my polyester socks smell?</summary><div class="a">Because polyester moves sweat without resisting the bacteria that feed on it. It wicks moisture to the surface and dries fast, but it has no natural odour resistance, unlike merino, so bacteria colonise the fabric and the smell builds up over repeated wears. <details class="faq"><summary>Can you wear wool socks in summer?</summary><div class="a">Yes — thin, lightweight merino, in a light knit. Merino regulates temperature both ways: the stored moisture evaporating cools the foot in heat, the same way it insulates in cold. Look for ultralight or no-show merino with mesh ventilation panels. Heavyweight wool and thick cotton are the wrong summer choices; thin merino and performance synthetics are the right ones.</div></details>
