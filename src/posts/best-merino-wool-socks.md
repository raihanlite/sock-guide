Merino wool is the fibre most sock guides reach for first, and for a simple reason: it does three jobs at once that other fibres only do one of. It absorbs around 30% of its weight in moisture before it feels wet, so feet stay feeling dry; it keeps insulating when damp, where cotton goes cold; and it naturally resists the odour-causing bacteria that thrive in sweaty shoes. That is why the same material shows up in hiking socks, running socks and everyday socks alike — the difference between the picks below is weight, height and cushion, not the fibre itself. The full fibre-by-fibre case is in [Cotton vs Merino Wool Socks](/posts/cotton-vs-merino-wool-socks/).

The short version: Darn Tough for the do-everything pair and the lightest hiker, Smartwool for hiking comfort and cold-weather running, Bombas for everyday wear, and Smartwool's thickest classic crew when the cold is the whole point. Look for a blend, not pure merino — 45–60% merino with nylon for durability and a little elastane for fit is the sweet spot most 2026 guides land on, since pure merino wears through faster. These six were picked from 2026 guides and maker specifications; no sock here was tested hands-on, and no testing is claimed.

## FAQ
<details class="faq"><summary>Are merino wool socks worth it?</summary><div class="a">If your feet get wet in use — work, sport, hiking, travel, long days — usually yes. You are paying for dry-feeling feet, fewer blisters and longer wear between washes, and the cost per wear drops fast when a pair lasts years: Darn Tough backs its socks for life, Smartwool for two years. For short, dry, everyday wear, cotton or a cotton blend does the same job for less. The full fibre comparison is in <a href="/posts/cotton-vs-merino-wool-socks/">Cotton vs Merino Wool Socks</a>.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Do merino socks keep feet warm when wet?</summary><div class="a">Yes — that is merino's biggest advantage over cotton. Merino fibres absorb moisture inside the fibre (around 30% of their weight before feeling wet) while still trapping insulating air, so the sock keeps warming your foot when damp. Cotton loses its insulation when wet and pulls heat away from the skin, which is why wet cotton socks feel cold. A thick merino sock in a roomy boot is the warmest combination here; a thin one still beats cotton in the wet.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>How do you wash merino wool socks?</summary><div class="a">Gentler than cotton, not complicated: machine wash cold or warm, ideally inside out, with a gentle detergent — no bleach, no fabric softener — and air dry instead of tumble drying on high heat. Heat is what shrinks and felts wool, so keep the dryer on low if you use it at all. The full routine for every material is in <a href="/posts/how-to-take-care-of-socks/">How to Take Care of Socks</a>.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Are merino wool socks itchy?</summary><div class="a">No, not the sock-grade kind. Merino fibres are fine enough to bend against the skin instead of prickling it, which is why merino base layers and socks are worn directly on skin by people who cannot tolerate ordinary wool. If a "wool" sock feels scratchy, it is usually a coarse wool blend, not merino — check the fibre content on the label.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>How long do merino wool socks last?</summary><div class="a">With care and rotation, quality merino socks last for years of regular use. The two things that kill them fastest are wearing the same pair day after day without rest, and high-heat drying. Rotate at least two or three pairs, wash cold and air dry, and replace a pair when the heel or toe goes thin or holes appear — the signs to watch for are in <a href="/posts/how-to-take-care-of-socks/">How to Take Care of Socks</a>.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Can you wear merino wool socks in summer?</summary><div class="a">Yes — match the weight to the season, not the fibre. A thin, lightweight merino sock breathes and wicks sweat, and often feels cooler than cotton in hot weather because it moves moisture away from the skin instead of holding it. The Light Hiker and the running pick above are the summer-friendly choices here; save the Extra Cushion Crew for cold days. More on summer socks in <a href="/posts/best-socks-for-summer/">What Are the Best Socks for Summer?</a>.</div></details>
