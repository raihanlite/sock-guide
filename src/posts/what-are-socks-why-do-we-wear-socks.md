A sock is a soft covering for the foot, usually worn inside a shoe. It sounds too simple to need explaining — and that is exactly why it is worth one. The sock is the layer that decides whether a shoe feels comfortable or punishing, whether feet stay dry or blister, and whether a pair of shoes lasts a season or years. This guide covers what a sock is, why we wear one, where socks came from, the main types, what they are made of, and how to look after them.

## What is a sock?

A sock is a knitted or woven covering for the foot, shaped to fit it and usually worn inside a shoe, covering the ankle or part of the calf. Most socks have the same parts: a cuff at the top that holds the sock up, a leg, a shaped heel pocket, a foot section, and a closed toe — often finished with a flat seam so it does not rub. The word itself is old: it comes from the Old English *socc*, from the Latin *soccus*, a light, low-heeled shoe worn by Roman comic actors. Over the centuries the meaning shifted from an outer shoe to the soft layer worn inside one.

## Why we wear socks

Socks do six jobs at once, and most of them are invisible until the sock is missing:

**Comfort and cushioning.** A sock adds a soft layer between foot and shoe, spreading pressure and absorbing some of the impact of every step — which matters most on long days standing or walking.

**Moisture management.** Your feet carry around a quarter of a million sweat glands between them, one of the densest concentrations anywhere on the body. A sock takes up that sweat — absorbing it or wicking it outward — so it does not pool against the skin. The full story of what happens when it cannot escape is in [Why Do Socks Make Your Feet Sweat?](/posts/why-do-socks-make-your-feet-sweat/)

**Friction and blisters.** Skin rubbing directly against a shoe lining is how blisters start. A sock puts a smooth, fitted layer in between, and a well-fitting sock moves with the foot instead of against it.

**Warmth.** A sock is an insulating layer: its fibres trap still air around the foot, and a thicker knit traps more. That is why the same foot can be warm in a wool crew and cold in a thin cotton ankle sock.

**Hygiene.** A sock you can wash takes the sweat, skin oils and bacteria that would otherwise go straight into the shoe — where they cannot be washed out, and where dampness feeds odour and fungal infections like athlete's foot.

**Protecting the shoe.** Shoes are the expensive, hard-to-clean half of the pair. By catching moisture and grit first, a sock keeps the lining drier and the shoe in service longer.

## A short history of the sock

The sock is one of the oldest garments we still wear, and for most of its history it was made by hand, one pair at a time.

The earliest foot coverings were animal skins wrapped around the foot and tied at the ankle. The oldest direct evidence comes from Ötzi the Iceman, who lived between about 3350 and 3105 BCE: his leather shoes were lined with a net of corded grass stuffed with hay — insulation, in effect, if not yet a sock.

By the 8th to 7th centuries BCE the Greek poet Hesiod was recommending felt socks called *piloi*, made of matted wool or fur, against the cold.

The oldest surviving socks were found in Egypt: dyed wool, made by nålebinding — a single-needle looping technique that predates true knitting — with a split toe so they could be worn with sandals. The best-known examples date to about 250–420 CE.

Knitting itself spread through the Middle East and Europe over the following centuries, and by the Middle Ages socks had become long, close-fitting leg coverings, held up with garters; fine silk stockings were a mark of wealth at courts such as Henry VIII's.

In 1589 the English clergyman William Lee invented the stocking frame, the first knitting machine. It knitted far faster than a person could by hand and became the prototype for the machines of the Industrial Revolution, when socks went from a hand-made luxury to a mass-produced everyday item.

Nylon arrived in 1938, giving socks enough stretch to stay up on their own — and making the sock garter, after centuries of service, obsolete.

## What socks are made from

The fibre decides what a sock does with sweat — and therefore most of what it feels like to wear.

**Cotton** — soft, cheap and comfortable while dry; but it absorbs sweat and holds it against the skin, so it stays damp and raises blister risk on long or active days.

**Merino wool** — fine wool that stores moisture inside the fibre, up to around 30% of its own weight before feeling wet; it regulates temperature in both directions and resists odour naturally.

**Synthetics (polyester, nylon)** — absorb almost no water, wick moisture outward and dry fastest; durable, but they hold odour more than wool over a long day.

**Blends** — most socks mix fibres to balance the trade-offs: merino with nylon for durability, cotton with polyester to dry faster, and a little elastane for stretch and shape retention.

The fibre-by-fibre comparison is in [Cotton vs Merino Wool Socks](/posts/cotton-vs-merino-wool-socks/).

## Looking after your socks

- **Wash cold or cool, inside out, and air-dry where possible.** High heat damages elastic and can shrink wool — the fastest way to kill a good pair is a hot tumble dryer.
- **Rotate pairs.** Give each pair a day's rest between wears so the knit recovers its shape, and change into a fresh pair when socks get damp or after a workout.
- **Replace them on condition, not on a calendar.** Thin heels and toes, holes, dead elastic and odour that survives washing are the signs. As a rough guide, everyday socks last about 6–12 months and athletic socks about 3–6 months.

## FAQ
<details class="faq"><summary>Why do we wear socks?</summary><div class="a">For comfort, dryness and protection, all at once: a sock cushions the foot, takes up sweat, stops the shoe rubbing the skin raw, adds warmth, and keeps the shoe itself cleaner. Any one of those jobs alone would justify it; together they are why almost everyone wears socks with closed shoes, every day, without thinking about it.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Can you wear shoes without socks?</summary><div class="a">You can, and with sandals or slip-on summer shoes many people do. In closed shoes it is usually a bad trade: sweat goes straight into the lining, where it cannot be washed out, the shoe becomes the damp, bacteria-friendly layer instead, and bare skin against the lining blisters more easily. A moisture-managing sock you can wash beats a shoe you cannot.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>How many pairs of socks should I own?</summary><div class="a">At least one pair for each day you wear shoes, plus spares — a week's worth is a practical minimum, and more if you exercise daily or work long shifts. Rotating pairs also makes each pair last longer, because the knit recovers its shape between wears.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Why do socks have heels?</summary><div class="a">Because feet do. The shaped heel pocket anchors the sock to the foot so it cannot twist or slide down inside the shoe. A sock without one — a simple tube — bunches at the ankle and rubs; the heel is what makes a sock stay where it is put.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>How old are socks?</summary><div class="a">The oldest surviving socks, found in Egypt, date to the first centuries CE, and foot coverings of skin and felt go back much further — to Ötzi the Iceman in about 3300 BCE and the felt piloi Hesiod recommended in the 8th to 7th centuries BCE. The full story is in the history section above.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Do I need different socks for different shoes?</summary><div class="a">Mainly it comes down to height and material. The sock should be taller than the shoe collar, so the collar rests on fabric rather than skin, and the fibre should match the activity — moisture-wicking for sport, warm for cold, thin for heat. The height trade-offs are laid out in <a href="/posts/crew-socks-vs-ankle-socks/">Crew Socks vs Ankle Socks</a>.</div></details>
