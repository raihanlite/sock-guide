The short answer is: during the day, and not at night.

Put compression socks on first thing in the morning, before swelling has a chance to build. Wear them while you are up and about, and take them off before bed. For most people that is roughly 8 to 12 hours.

The exact schedule depends on why you wear them — a long flight, a standing shift and a post-surgery recovery all set different windows. And if a clinician has prescribed compression, their schedule overrides everything in this guide.

## The daily routine

The routine Cleveland Clinic's doctors describe is simple: **on in the morning, off before bed.**

After a night lying flat, gravity has not had hours to pool blood in the leg veins, so your legs are at their least swollen in the morning. The socks go on more easily and fit as intended.

Wear them through the day while you are sitting, standing or walking. That is when gravity pulls fluid toward the feet and ankles and compression does its job.

Take them off to shower, and take them off at least once a day to wash your legs and check your skin. That daily skin check matters: a sock that is too tight, or skin that is irritated underneath it, is only visible when the sock comes off.

There is no single number of hours that suits everyone. The right way to think about it is the shortest, simplest schedule that keeps your symptoms under control without creating new ones.

If your legs feel better with the socks on and unchanged when they come off at night, the schedule is working.

If discomfort grows over the day, or symptoms return the moment the socks come off, the schedule or the fit needs a second look — not more hours.

## Can you sleep in compression socks?

Usually, no. Compression socks work by fighting gravity, and when you are lying down, gravity is no longer pulling blood down into your legs — so there is nothing left for the socks to fight, and your skin loses its recovery window for no gain. Cleveland Clinic's advice is plain: besides the occasional catnap, you should not sleep in them, and wearing them nonstop is not great for your skin.

The exceptions are all clinician-directed: clot prevention after surgery, lymphedema management, and some leg-ulcer care, where a doctor may specifically prescribe overnight wear. If a doctor has told you to wear them overnight, follow that schedule exactly — and note that night-specific compression garments exist for prescribed overnight use, built differently from a standard graduated sock. If you simply want something on your feet at night, a soft, non-binding sock is the better choice.

## Starting out: build up gradually

If you have never worn compression socks, do not start with a full day.

A common build-up is 2 to 4 hours a day for the first few days, then 4 to 6 hours over the rest of the first week if they feel comfortable, and a full day from the second week onward.

The first days tell you whether the size is right: a sock that is correct feels snug and even, not painful, and the top band should not dig in.

Discomfort that fades as you get used to the feeling is normal. Pain, numbness or cold feet are not, and are a reason to take them off and reconsider the size or the strength.

How many weeks or months you need them depends on the reason. For travel, it is the flight. After surgery, your surgeon will usually say how long, often several weeks. For chronic conditions such as varicose veins or venous insufficiency, compression is often a long-term, daily habit. What compression socks are for, and what the strength numbers mean, is in [What Are Compression Socks Used For?](/posts/what-are-compression-socks-used-for/).

## Signs to take them off sooner

Take the socks off and seek medical advice if you notice pain, pins-and-needles or numbness, cold feet, toes that look pale, blue or unusually dark, deep skin marks that do not fade, or swelling that gets worse while wearing them.

A sock that cuts into the skin, bunches at the ankle or slides down is not doing its job. It is delivering uneven pressure, and uneven pressure can worsen discomfort.

These signs are also the test for whether the size is right: a well-fitted compression sock feels snug and even all day, and the legs feel better with it on than without it.

## Washing and care

Compression comes from elastic fibres, and the makers name the same threats to them: heat, harsh chemicals and stretching. The routine, drawn from the makers' own care pages, is:

1. Turn the socks inside out and put them in a mesh laundry bag.
2. Wash on a gentle cycle with a mild detergent, at the temperature your maker states — cold or cool suits most, warm is allowed by some. No bleach, no fabric softener: softener coats the fibres and reduces elasticity.
3. Roll them in a towel and press the water out. Do not wring or twist them.
4. Dry them flat or hung, away from radiators and direct sun. Some makers allow low-heat tumble drying, others say avoid the dryer entirely — your own label decides.

Wash them often, ideally after each wearing. Oils and sweat break the elastic down, so regular washing actually helps the socks last. One maker notes its stockings regain their original elasticity after washing and drying.

The general care principles — cold water, rotation, replacing on condition — are the same as in [How to Take Care of Socks](/posts/how-to-take-care-of-socks/). Compression socks are simply less forgiving when the care is skipped, because the thing that wears out is the pressure itself.

## When to replace them

Even with perfect care, compression socks wear out. With daily wear, most need replacing every **3 to 6 months**.

The clearest signs it is time for a new pair: the sock no longer feels snug, it is easier to put on than it used to be, it slides down or bunches at the ankle, or it stops springing back into shape after a wash.

Thinning fabric at the heel or toe, holes or runs, and a cuff that stays stretched are the same message in visible form.

If swelling or tiredness returns while you are wearing a pair that used to work, that is a strong sign the compression level has dropped below what it was. Replace the pair rather than wearing it longer.

Keep a rotation of two or three pairs, so one is always clean and the elastic in each gets a rest between wears.

A note on this page itself: it is general information about wearing compression socks, not medical advice. If compression has been prescribed for a medical reason, the wearing schedule — including any overnight wear — comes from the clinician who prescribed it, not from a buying guide.
