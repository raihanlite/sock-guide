A hand-knitted sock is the same object as a factory sock — a tube with a heel pocket and a closed toe. The difference is the route: you build it stitch by stitch on needles, shaping the heel and closing the toe as part of the knitting, while a factory knits the whole sock on a circular machine in under a minute.

It is slower, and that is the point: you control the fit, the length and the yarn, and you end up with a pair made to your feet rather than to a size chart.

This guide is a plain-language overview of the process, not a pattern. It explains what each part of a sock is, the order the parts are knitted in, and the decisions a beginner has to make. For stitch counts, heel-turn rows and the shaping schedule, use a complete sock pattern — the numbers depend on your gauge and foot size, and no overview can supply them.

## Before you start: yarn, needles and gauge

Three things decide whether a hand-knitted sock works: the yarn, the needles, and the gauge — the number of stitches per inch. Get those right and the rest is following the pattern one section at a time.

### Yarn

The classic sock yarn is fingering weight, also called sock weight or 4-ply: fine yarn sold in 100-gram skeins of roughly 400 metres.

One skein is enough for an adult pair of average size — about 350 to 420 metres for most adults, more for large feet or long legs.

The standard blend is around 75% superwash wool and 25% nylon: the wool gives warmth and spring, the nylon gives the heels and toes the strength to survive walking.

Superwash wool matters for a first pair, because it can be machine washed; untreated wool felts.

A thicker yarn (sport or DK weight) knits faster and is tempting for a first pair, but it makes a bulkier sock that fits less easily inside shoes — save it for house socks and boot socks.

### Needles

Socks are knitted in the round on a small circumference, so you need one of three setups: a set of four or five double-pointed needles (DPNs), one long circular needle for magic loop, or two short circulars.

All three work. DPNs are the traditional choice and the one most beginner patterns are written for.

The usual size for fingering-weight sock yarn is US 0 to US 2 (2.0 to 2.75 mm), most often US 1 (2.25 mm). Wood or bamboo needles grip the fine yarn and stop stitches sliding off, which is kinder to a beginner than slick metal.

### Gauge

Most fingering-weight sock patterns are written for about 7 to 8 stitches per inch in stockinette stitch — roughly 32 stitches over 4 inches.

Knit a swatch in the round on the needles you plan to use, wash and dry it the way you will wash the socks, and count.

Socks are knitted tighter than a sweater in the same yarn, because a firm fabric lasts longer underfoot.

A knitted sock is also made with negative ease: its unstretched circumference is usually about 5 to 10% smaller than the foot, so the fabric stays in contact with the skin instead of sliding around inside the shoe.

If your swatch is looser than the pattern gauge, the sock will come out too big no matter how carefully you follow the rest.

You also need a blunt tapestry needle for grafting the toe, a stitch marker for the start of the round, and a row counter or a notebook — heel-flap rows, gusset decreases and foot rows all have to match between the two socks, and counting is how the second sock comes out the same as the first.

## Cuff-down vs toe-up: two directions, same sock

The first decision is which end to start from. A cuff-down sock starts at the cuff and works towards the toe; a toe-up sock starts at the toe and works towards the cuff. The finished sock has the same parts either way — cuff, leg, heel, foot, toe — in the opposite order.

Neither direction is better. Most knitters try both and pick a favourite, and patterns specify which they use — switching direction means reworking the heel and the cast-on, which in practice means choosing a different pattern. For a first pair, cuff-down is the usual recommendation, because the progression feels intuitive: a familiar ribbed cuff, a plain leg, then the heel, then the foot. The rest of this guide follows the cuff-down order.

## The cuff and the leg

The cuff is the top of the sock, usually ribbed in knit 1, purl 1 or knit 2, purl 2, so it grips the leg and keeps the sock up.

Cast on with a stretchy cast-on — long-tail works for most knitters — join the round without twisting the cast-on edge, place a marker for the start of the round, and work the rib for roughly 1 to 2 inches.

Check that the cast-on edge stretches over your heel and settles comfortably around your leg; a larger cast-on needle alone does not guarantee this.

Then comes the leg: plain stockinette stitch, or a simple pattern, worked in the round until the leg reaches the length you want. An ankle sock might have a 3 to 4 inch leg after the cuff; a crew sock is longer.

The type-by-type guide to lengths is in [Types of Socks](/posts/types-of-socks/) — the leg is where you decide which one you are making.

Knitting the leg is the easy, rhythmic part of the sock, and it is also where a first knitter learns to keep an even tension in the round.

## Turning the heel

The heel is the part that makes socks look intimidating, and it is the part where most of the variety between patterns lives. A sock is a tube; the heel is what bends the tube into an L-shape that fits a foot. There are two main ways to do it.

### Heel flap and gusset — the classic

After the leg, the sock is divided in half.

One half of the stitches — the heel stitches — are worked back and forth in rows to make the heel flap, a flat rectangle about 2 to 3 inches long, usually in a slip-stitch pattern that makes the fabric denser and longer-wearing.

The first stitch of each row is slipped, which leaves a neat chain edge for picking up stitches later. The other half of the stitches, the instep, waits untouched on a holder.

Then comes the heel turn: a series of short rows — rows that stop before the end, turn, and come back — with decreases that shape the flat flap into a little cup that sits under the heel bone. The heel turn looks wrong until the last row. Follow the pattern literally and do not rip it back halfway; the cup only appears when the short rows are complete.

With the cup formed, stitches are picked up along both edges of the flap — under both loops of each slipped-stitch chain, so there are no holes at the corners — and the sock is joined back into the round.

There are now more stitches than the leg had, so the extra stitches are decreased away every other round along the sides of the foot.

That wedge-shaped area is the gusset, and it gives the sock room through the heel-to-instep transition. Once the stitch count is back to the leg count, the heel is done.

### Short-row heel — the simpler alternative

A short-row heel skips the flap and the gusset entirely: the heel is shaped with short rows alone, worked in the round, and the sock goes straight on to the foot.

It is quicker and uses less yarn, and it works in either direction, cuff-down or toe-up.

Some knitters find it fits less well than a flap-and-gusset heel, because there is no gusset to give the instep room; others prefer it for exactly that simplicity.

A third option, the afterthought heel, knits the whole sock as a straight tube and adds the heel afterwards — a useful trick, but not the one to learn first.

Whichever heel you knit, try the sock on once it is complete and again before the toe shaping. The heel cup should sit under the heel bone, the sock should fit snugly without pulling hard across the instep, and the toes should not be crushed.

## The foot and the toe

After the gusset decreases, the foot is another plain tube. Knit every stitch of every round until the foot is about 2 inches (5 cm) shorter than the total foot length you need, measured from the back of the heel to the tip of the longest toe.

Then the toe decreases begin: usually four decreases in a round, worked every other round until about half the stitches have gone, then every round until only a handful of stitches remain.

The decreases are paired — knit two together on one side, slip-slip-knit on the other — so they lean the right way on each side of the foot.

Starting the toe at the right point is the difference between a sock that fits and one that bunches in the shoe. Too early and the toe is short and tight; too late and the extra length folds over the toes. This is the second fitting point: try the sock on before the decreases start.

## Grafting the toe and finishing

The last stitches are closed with the Kitchener stitch, named after Lord Kitchener, who had it used for military socks in the First World War because it leaves no seam to rub against the foot.

It is grafting, not knitting: the working yarn is cut, threaded on a tapestry needle, and sewn through the live stitches on the two needles — front and back — in a set sequence that mimics the path of a knitted stitch.

Done at the right tension, the join is invisible and the toe has no ridge at all. A bent-tip tapestry needle helps, because it slides through the live stitches more easily than a straight one.

The simpler alternative is a gathered close: thread the yarn through the remaining stitches and draw them together like the top of a hat. It is quicker, but it leaves a small pucker, so most sock patterns specify the Kitchener stitch.

### Finishing the pair

Then weave in the ends, give the socks a gentle wash, and let them dry flat.

How you wash them from here decides how long they keep their shape — the full routine is in [How to Take Care of Socks](/posts/how-to-take-care-of-socks/), and hand-knitted wool needs the gentlest version of it unless the yarn is superwash.

Weigh the first sock before you start the second: if it weighs more than half the skein, buy another skein from the same dye lot before you begin, or the second sock will run out of yarn.

That, and knitting both socks at the same time on one long circular needle, are the two cures for second sock syndrome — the very real phenomenon where the first sock sits alone in a drawer forever.

A hand-knitted sock is the oldest way socks were made. Knitting spread through Europe from the Middle Ages, and the first knitting machine — William Lee's stocking frame of 1589 — was invented to copy what hand-knitters already did; the story is in [History of Socks](/posts/history-of-socks/). The factory version is described in [How Socks Are Made](/posts/how-socks-are-made/) — same object, same parts, built by machine instead of by hand.

## Common beginner mistakes

**Skipping the swatch.** Gauge is the whole fit. A sock knitted at 6 stitches per inch instead of 8 is a quarter wider than the pattern intends, and no amount of careful heel work fixes it.

**Needles too big.** A loose, drapey fabric feels soft in the hand and wears through underfoot in weeks. Sock fabric should feel firm and dense.

**Twisting the cast-on join.** If the cast-on edge is twisted when you join the round, the cuff will have a permanent twist in it. Lay the stitches flat and check twice before the first round.

**Ladders between needles.** On double-pointed needles, the first stitch on each needle tends to loosen, leaving a vertical line of loose stitches — a ladder. Pull the first stitch of each needle snug, and shift the stitches around the needles every few rounds so the joins move.

**Ripping back the heel turn.** The heel turn looks like a mistake until the final short rows. Trust the pattern and finish the section before judging it.

**Not counting.** Heel-flap rows, gusset pickups, foot rows and toe decreases all have to match between the two socks. A row counter or a written note is not optional — it is how the pair comes out as a pair.

**The wrong yarn for the job.** Pure cotton and pure alpaca have little elasticity and no nylon, so they sag and wear faster in a sock. That is why most sock yarn is a wool-and-nylon blend, and why the 75% wool / 25% nylon mix is the default, not the exception.
