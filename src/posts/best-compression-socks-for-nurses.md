A nursing shift is twelve hours on your feet — walking the ward, standing at a bedside, lifting, turning, and rarely sitting down long enough for your legs to recover. By the last hour, most nurses know the feeling: heavy calves, swollen ankles, feet that ache inside the shoe. That is the exact job compression socks were made for. A graduated compression sock squeezes hardest at the ankle and eases off up the calf, helping blood that has pooled in the lower legs get back to the heart instead of sitting there all shift — which is why the standard benefits Cleveland Clinic lists are less swelling, relief for tired or heavy legs, and support for vein health. What nurses need from that sock, though, is different from what a traveller or a runner needs: graduated compression that holds up for a full shift, a sock that is easy to pull on at 6 a.m. and easy to get off at 7 p.m., and a knit that survives being washed after every shift without going baggy at the ankle. The background on how graduated compression works and what the mmHg numbers mean is in [What Are Compression Socks Used For?](/posts/what-are-compression-socks-used-for/), and the wearing rules — how long to keep them on, and when to take them off — are in [How Long Can You Wear Compression Socks?](/posts/how-long-can-you-wear-compression-socks/).

This guide covers the two levels nurses actually buy: **15–20 mmHg**, the mild level sold over the counter and the usual starting point, and **20–30 mmHg**, the firm level. One line before the picks: **20–30 mmHg and above is a medical decision to discuss with a clinician.** In head-to-head trials on prolonged standing, Nightingale Knows (September 2026) found 15–20 mmHg matched 20–30 mmHg for fatigue, swelling and discomfort — the firmer level is for pronounced edema, early varicose veins, pregnancy swelling, or a clinician's advice, and 30–40 mmHg is prescription territory. Fitted medical-grade socks (Jobst, Sigvaris, Mediven) are the answer for venous disease, a history of deep vein thrombosis, or post-surgical wear — not a shop shelf. So this guide picks by shift need: the best all-round sock, the best merino sock, the best firm budget sock, the best bulk buy for a rotation, the best cushioned sock, and the best wide-calf sock. For sweaty feet on shift, the moisture-wicking picks are in [Best Socks for Sweaty Feet](/posts/best-socks-for-sweaty-feet/), the everyday rotation pairs with them in [Best Everyday Socks](/posts/best-everyday-socks/), and the washing rules that keep compression working are in [How to Take Care of Socks](/posts/how-to-take-care-of-socks/).

The short version: Comrad's CloudCotton for the best all-round shift sock, Sockwell's Circulator for the best merino feel, Physix Gear for firm 20–30 mmHg on a budget, CHARMKING's three-pack for the cheapest rotation, Bombas for the most cushioned pair, and Vim &amp; Vigr's merino wide-calf for calves that standard sizes squeeze. One fit rule decides most of it: measure your calf at its widest point in the morning, before swelling starts, and buy the size the maker's chart gives for that number — a compression sock that is too tight at the calf cuts circulation instead of helping it, and one that is too loose slides down and bunches at the ankle.

## FAQ
<details class="faq"><summary>What compression level do nurses need?</summary><div class="a">For most nurses, 15–20 mmHg is the right level: it is sold over the counter, it is the level most 2026 guides recommend first, and in head-to-head trials on prolonged standing it matched 20–30 mmHg for fatigue, swelling and discomfort. Go to 20–30 mmHg for pronounced swelling, early varicose veins, pregnancy swelling, or a clinician's advice — and 30–40 mmHg is prescription territory. The full level ladder is in <a href="/posts/what-are-compression-socks-used-for/">What Are Compression Socks Used For?</a>.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Can nurses wear compression socks every day?</summary><div class="a">Yes — for the daytime shift. Put them on in the morning, when the legs are least swollen, and take them off when the shift ends; compression socks are generally not worn overnight, and sleeping in them is a decision for a clinician, not a habit. Wash them after every shift, cold and gentle, and rotate at least three pairs so each one recovers its shape between wears. The wearing rules are in <a href="/posts/how-long-can-you-wear-compression-socks/">How Long Can You Wear Compression Socks?</a>.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>How long do compression socks last?</summary><div class="a">About three to six months of daily shift wear — sooner than ordinary socks, because the elastic that delivers the compression is the part that wears out. Replace a pair when the cuff slides down, the sock bunches at the ankle, the heel or toe thins, or the legs feel the same with the sock on as without it. Hot water, bleach, fabric softener and tumble-drying all shorten the life; the care rules are in <a href="/posts/how-to-take-care-of-socks/">How to Take Care of Socks</a>.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Are compression socks hot to wear on a 12-hour shift?</summary><div class="a">They can be, in a thick cotton knit on a warm ward — but the merino and bamboo blends on this page (Sockwell Circulator, Vim &amp; Vigr Checkmate) breathe and wick moisture far better than a plain cotton compression sock, and the full-synthetic Physix Gear and CHARMKING picks dry fast. If your feet sweat through a shift, the moisture-wicking picks are in <a href="/posts/best-socks-for-sweaty-feet/">Best Socks for Sweaty Feet</a>, and the fibre-by-fibre comparison is in <a href="/posts/sock-fabrics/">Sock Fabrics</a>.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>How do I get compression socks on and off easily?</summary><div class="a">Turn the sock inside out down to the heel, slide the foot in, then roll the rest up the leg a little at a time — never yank it up by the cuff, which stretches the knit unevenly. Put them on in the morning, before the legs swell, and a pair of rubber gloves gives grip on the fabric without snagging it. Taking them off is the reverse: peel them down from the top rather than pulling from the toe. The same technique is shown in the video above.</div></details>

<details class="faq"><summary>Do I need a prescription for compression socks for nursing?</summary><div class="a">Not for the lighter levels: anything under 20 mmHg is normally sold over the counter. At 20 mmHg and above, compression socks are generally a prescription item, and the level, fit and wearing schedule are set by a clinician. If a clinician has told you to wear compression, that prescription — not a shop shelf — is the authority.</div></details>

A note on this page itself: it is general information about compression socks, not medical advice. If compression has been recommended for a medical reason, the level, the fit and the wearing schedule come from the clinician who recommended it, not from a buying guide.
